# Partnerships in compartmental STI models: a literature review (decision 2, S3.7)

A review for decision 2 in [plan.md](plan.md): should v4 keep, change, or remove the partnership approximation that runs `ss.MFNet` and `ss.PartnerNet(dur=)` as compartments? It builds on [oct20/simplify/partnerships.md](../oct20/simplify/partnerships.md), which has the measurements against agents. Sources are marked **[checked]** when I read the paper, its abstract, or its code this session, and **[memory]** when the citation is from memory only (standard references I am confident of, but did not open). Nothing in `starsim/` was changed.

## Summary and recommendation

**Starsim's approximation is the most common convention in compartmental STI models, not an invention.** Almost every widely used compartmental STI or HIV model writes the force of infection as a rate of acquiring partners (`c`) times a probability that a partnership with an infected person transmits (`β` per partnership), times the prevalence among the partners one picks: Anderson and May's and Garnett's partner-change models, Elbasha's HPV model, Whittles' gonorrhoea model (`gonovax`), Thembisa's short-term partnerships, and the chlamydia evidence synthesis of Lewis, White and Price. Starsim's `φ_i = n_i/D̄` is that partner-change rate, and its `P = H/(H + c + 1/D̄)` is the per-partnership probability, computed from acts per unit time, the hazard per act, and how long the partnership lasts rather than given. That formula is the one the pair-model literature uses for the chance a discordant partnership transmits (Kretzschmar and Heijne's primer writes `β/(σ + 2μ + β + γ)`, Starsim's form exactly). What the earlier note called "in neither literature" is really the classical model with its per-partnership probability derived the way pair models derive it.

**The alternatives are less common in practice.** Two other conventions are used by major models: acts as contacts with no partnerships (Optima HIV, EpiModel's `dcm`, Goals as far as I can tell), which is much more biased than Starsim's form for long partnerships (3.9 to 7.4 times too high at 20 years in the earlier measurements), and pair-formation models with partnerships as compartments (Dietz and Hadeler, Kretzschmar and Dietz, Heijne and colleagues for chlamydia, Leng and Keeling), which are more accurate for monogamy but used mostly in research models, not in the frameworks that run country analyses. Thembisa mixes the two: short-term partnerships the classical way, and marriages as a person-level state (married, and the partner's risk group) with monthly acts, but not with the spouse's infection status. Pair approximation and moment closure for concurrency (Bauch and Rand, Eames and Keeling) stay in the research literature.

**The known biases are the ones Starsim's spec already states.** Comparisons of classical and pair or network models (Lloyd-Smith et al. 2004; Ong et al. 2012; Johnson and Geffen 2016; Heijne et al. 2011) all find the same thing: the classical form is fine when partnerships are short against the infection, or for chronic infections with low transmissibility (HIV, roughly), and is wrong, in either direction, for curable STIs with partnerships about as long as the infection. Calibration hides some of this for HIV, but not for gonorrhoea and chlamydia (Ong et al.), and fitted parameters and intervention effects differ (Johnson and Geffen).

**Recommendation: keep the approximation (option 3 of the earlier note), and describe it as the standard partner-change model.** Reasons:

1. It is the convention of most published compartmental STI models, so a modeler who writes `solver='ode'` with `ss.MFNet` gets the model they would expect to write by hand, with `c` and `β` per partnership worked out from the network's `n_contacts`, `dur`, and `acts` and the disease's beta per act. Removing it would leave only the acts-as-contacts form, which is the more biased of the two common conventions for long partnerships.
2. Its errors are the known errors of that convention, and they are documented and measured in spec 12. `auto` keeps agents, so nobody gets it without asking.
3. A pair model is the accurate alternative for monogamy and is well established, but it is a different model (pairs as compartments, squared in the number of states), used by research models rather than by the major frameworks. It belongs in the library as a NumPy component if a project needs it, as the earlier note said, not in the engine.

Small changes worth making if it stays (each is a few lines): name it in `describe()`, the docs, and the spec as "the partner-change (Anderson–May) model, with the chance per partnership from acts and duration" so it is recognizable; say in the note that it is too high for curable STIs with short infections and that concurrency is ignored (already in spec 06, so only the wording); and consider letting a user give the probability per partnership directly (as gonovax and the HPV models do), which is the common parameter when acts are not known. Whether this is worth about 230 lines is Cliff's call on scope (principle 10), not a question the literature settles; the literature says it is a standard and defensible model, not an idiosyncratic one.

If the line count matters more, removing it is defensible too, provided the refusal points to the acts-as-contacts recipe and says plainly that it is more biased for long partnerships. I would not replace it with a pair model in the engine.

## What Starsim does now

Under a compartmental solver (`ode`, `tau`, `gillespie`), `ss.solvers.Mixing.partnerships()` (`starsim/solvers/mixing.py`, about line 212) turns a network of lasting partnerships (`ss.MFNet`, or `ss.PartnerNet` with `dur=`) into a contact matrix: a person in cell `i` (a side of the network, such as sex, by a level of the dimension it counts or matches partners by) forms partnerships at `φ_i = n_i/D̄`, partners at a time over the mean duration, with partners drawn at random from the other side in proportion to their partners (balanced so the side with fewer partners sets the total), and with `match=` a share within the same level. `Compartmental.partnership_chance()` (`starsim/solvers/compartmental.py`, about line 1175) gives each new partnership with someone in a source state the chance it transmits before it ends, the partner leaves that state, or the person leaves theirs: `P = H/(H + c + 1/D̄)` for durations at a constant rate, or `H/(H + c)·(1 − E[exp(−(H + c)·T)])` over 32 points of another duration distribution, where `H` is acts per unit time times the hazard per act in that direction and `c` is the rate of those exits. The force of infection is `λ_i = φ_i·Σ_j m_ij·Σ_s w_s·P_ij,s·I_j,s/N_j`, worked out when the run starts, with `rel_trans`, `rel_sus` and modifiers multiplying `P`. It leaves out partnerships under way at the start and pairs that stay together after one partner is infected (so it ignores that infection stays within pairs, and that concurrency spreads it faster). It is marked `approx`, `auto` runs these networks as agents (`solvers/choose.py`), and spec [06-routes.md](../design/06-routes.md) ("Networks of partnerships") and [12-backends.md](../design/12-backends.md) give its measured ratios to agents: about 1.0 for 6-month partnerships, 1.11 and 1.25 at 10 and 20 years for 5-year monogamous HIV-like partnerships, and 0.19 and 0.13 for a gonorrhea-like infection with Poisson concurrency.

## Approaches

| Approach | What it captures | What it misses | Who uses it | Key citation |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Acts (or contacts) as mass action, no partnerships | Acts between groups, condoms, per-act probability by stage; mixing between groups | Partnerships altogether: every act is with a new partner at the population's prevalence, so repeated acts with the same (uninfected) partner count as fresh exposures; strongly biased high for long partnerships | Optima HIV, EpiModel `dcm`, Goals (probably) | Kerr et al. 2015 [checked] |
| Partner change rate times a probability per partnership (classical, frequency-dependent) | Number of partners, activity groups, assortative and age mixing; saturation within a partnership through `β` per partnership (from `1 − (1 − p)^n` over acts, or given) | Partnership duration as a period of protection or trapping, reinfection within a pair, concurrency, partners' infection status | Anderson–May and Garnett tradition; Elbasha HPV; gonovax (Whittles); Thembisa (short-term partnerships); most deterministic chlamydia models; Starsim's approximation | Garnett and Anderson 1994; Elbasha et al. 2007 [checked] |
| Same, with the per-partnership probability from competing risks over the duration | As above, with `P = H/(H + σ + exits)` so long partnerships saturate and short ones do not | As above | Starsim; the pair-model literature uses the same expression for `R0` and for the chance a discordant pair transmits | Kretzschmar and Heijne 2017 [checked] |
| Partnership type as a person state, without partner's infection status | Marriage as a lasting state with its own act rate; concurrency propensity as a risk group | Within-pair saturation (each month's acts are with a spouse drawn at the married population's prevalence) | Thembisa (marital partnerships) | Johnson and Dorrington 2025 [checked] |
| Pair formation (pairs as compartments) | Singles and pairs by both partners' states; formation, separation, transmission and reinfection within pairs; duration and gaps between partners | Concurrency (strict serial monogamy), unless extended; state space squares | Dietz and Hadeler; Kretzschmar and Dietz; Heijne et al. chlamydia; Leng and Keeling; Ong et al. | Dietz and Hadeler 1988; Kretzschmar and Dietz 1998 [checked] |
| Pair formation plus instantaneous casual contacts | Steady pairs plus casual contacts for people in and out of pairs (a simple concurrency) | Concurrency among steady partnerships | Xiridou et al. (per the primer); Leng and Keeling's third model | Leng and Keeling 2018 [checked] |
| Pair approximation and moment closure on a dynamic network | Concurrency and degree heterogeneity, through counts of pairs by state with closure of triples | Accuracy depends on the closure; complex; rarely used for policy | Research models | Bauch and Rand 2000; Eames and Keeling 2002 [checked] |
| Individual-based network | Everything above, and exact partner identity | Speed; needs agents | EMOD, HIV Synthesis, EpiModel `netsim`, HPV-ADVISE, STIsim (all out of scope) | — |

## Notes by model and framework

**Anderson and May, Garnett (classical partner-change models).** The founding compartmental STI framework: people stratified by sexual activity (rate of acquiring new partners `c`), with `β` the probability of transmission per partnership and `R0 ≈ β·c·D` adjusted for the variance of `c` [memory: Anderson and May 1991, *Infectious Diseases of Humans*, Oxford University Press]. Garnett and Anderson balance the partnerships of the two sexes across age and activity groups by adjusting the rates at which each group acquires partners (Garnett GP, Anderson RM 1994, "Balancing sexual partnerships in an age and activity stratified model of HIV transmission in heterosexual populations", *IMA J Math Appl Med Biol* 11(3):161–192, doi:10.1093/imammb/11.3.161) [checked: abstract]. Garnett's introduction for non-mathematicians is the standard overview (Garnett GP 2002, "An introduction to mathematical models in sexually transmitted disease epidemiology", *Sex Transm Infect* 78(1):7–12) [checked: citation and abstract only]. Starsim's balancing (the side with fewer partners sets the total) is one of the choices this literature discusses.

**Dietz and Hadeler; Kretzschmar and Dietz (pair formation).** Dietz and Hadeler add a pairing rate and a separation rate to the classical model; a pair of susceptibles is protected while it lasts, endemic states need a high enough separation rate, and the classical model is the limit of infinitely fast separation (Dietz K, Hadeler KP 1988, "Epidemiological models for sexually transmitted diseases", *J Math Biol* 26:1–25) [checked: abstract]. Kretzschmar and Dietz extend this to HIV with variable infectivity (Kretzschmar M, Dietz K 1998, "The effect of pair formation and variable infectivity on the spread of an infection without recovery", *Math Biosci* 148:83–113) [checked: citation only]. The `R0` of a pair model, with a partnership transmitting at most once, is from Diekmann, Dietz and Heesterbeek [memory: 1991, "The basic reproduction ratio for sexually transmitted diseases: I. Theoretical considerations", *Math Biosci* 107:325–339]. Kretzschmar and Heijne's primer is the clearest modern statement: classical models assume "contacts are instantaneous and every contact is with another individual of the population", which is adequate when "pair formation and separation dynamics are fast compared to transmission dynamics"; a discordant pair transmits with `b = β/(σ + 2μ + β + γ)`; and concurrency needs added instantaneous contacts, pair approximation, or simulation (Kretzschmar M, Heijne JCM 2017, "Pair formation models for sexually transmitted infections: A primer", *Infect Dis Model* 2(3):368–378, PMC6002071) [checked].

**Heijne, Althaus and colleagues (chlamydia pair model).** A pair compartmental model adapted from Kretzschmar and Dietz, with partnership duration and reinfection within partnerships, predicts a smaller effect of screening than an instantaneous-contact model calibrated to the same prevalence, and argues for partner management (Heijne JCM, Althaus CL, Herzog SA, Kretzschmar M, Low N 2011, "The role of reinfection and partner notification in the efficacy of Chlamydia screening programs", *J Infect Dis* 203(3):372–377, PMC3071108) [checked: abstract]. Their later partner-notification work is individual-based.

**Comparisons of classical and pair or network models.** Lloyd-Smith, Getz and Westerhoff derive frequency-dependent incidence from a pair model and find it more accurate for chronic, less transmissible infections than for brief, highly transmissible ones (2004, "Frequency-dependent incidence in models of sexually transmitted diseases: portrayal of pair-based transmission and effects of illness on contact behaviour", *Proc R Soc B* 271:625–634, PMC1691637) [checked: abstract]. Ong, Fu, Lee and Chen compare deterministic pair and classical models for gonorrhoea, chlamydia, and HIV: they diverge for partnerships longer than about 10 days, in either direction depending on partnership and gap lengths; calibration does not resolve the difference for gonorrhoea and chlamydia (it needs implausible durations of infection) but nearly does for HIV's critical condom use (2012, "Comparability of results from pair and classical model formulations for different sexually transmitted infections", *PLoS One* 7(6):e39575, PMC3384672) [checked]. Johnson and Geffen compare a deterministic frequency-dependent approximation with a microsimulation network model of six STIs in South Africa, using the same behavior data: the frequency-dependent model gives higher prevalence for all six (most for curable STIs), different fitted parameters, smaller effects of removing concurrency and commercial sex, and a larger effect of prevention within marriages (2016, "A comparison of two mathematical modeling frameworks for evaluating sexually transmitted infection epidemiology", *Sex Transm Dis* 43(3):139–146, doi:10.1097/OLQ.0000000000000412) [checked: abstract].

**Pair approximation and moment closure.** Bauch and Rand derive equations for singletons and pairs from a stochastic process of partnership formation and transmission, closing higher-order correlations by a pair approximation, and study final size against Kretzschmar and Morris's concurrency index (2000, "A moment closure model for sexually transmitted disease transmission through a concurrent partnership network", *Proc R Soc B* 267:2019–2027, PMC1690763) [checked: abstract]. Eames and Keeling's pairwise model handles heterogeneous degree and dynamic partnerships and agrees closely with stochastic simulations; network structure matters most when the mean number of partners is small, as for many STIs (2002, "Modeling dynamic and network heterogeneities in the spread of sexually transmitted diseases", *PNAS* 99(20):13330–13335, PMC130633) [checked: abstract]. Leng and Keeling nest three pair-formation SIS models (stable pairs only; plus casual contacts between singles; plus casual contacts for people in pairs) and find that concurrency raises prevalence and the vaccination needed at a fixed transmission rate, but matters much less once each model is fitted to the same prevalence (2018, "Concurrency of partnerships, consistency with data, and control of sexually transmitted infections", *Epidemics* 25:35–46) [checked: abstract]. This last result is the best argument that a calibrated classical model is often good enough.

**Per-partnership probability from acts (the binomial adjustment).** The usual way to get `β` per partnership is `1 − (1 − p)^n` over `n` acts, or `1 − exp(−n·h)` with a hazard per act. Thembisa does exactly this (below). Lewis, White and Price argue that the probability per partnership is the more useful parameter for chlamydia, since partner numbers are measured better than acts, and estimate it from Natsal-2 and NHANES with an SIS model whose force of infection is the rate of partnerships with infected people times that probability: about 32% to 35% male to female, and 5% to 21% female to male, very uncertain (Lewis J, White PJ, Price MJ 2021, "Per-partnership transmission probabilities for *Chlamydia trachomatis* infection: evidence synthesis of population-based survey data", *Int J Epidemiol* 50(2):510–517, PMC8128448) [checked]. Starsim's form differs from the fixed-`n` binomial in two ways: it averages over the distribution of durations (`E[1 − exp(−H·T)]` rather than `1 − exp(−H·E[T])`, which is lower for the same mean), and it lets the partnership stop being a risk when the partner recovers or dies. Both are refinements in the same convention, not a different model. I did not confirm the binomial form in Boily's Cotonou model (Boily M-C, Lowndes C, Alary M 2002, "The impact of HIV epidemic phases on the effectiveness of core group interventions: insights from mathematical models", *Sex Transm Infect* 78(Suppl 1):i78–i90) [checked: citation and abstract only], or in Goodreau's work, which I know mainly as network models; I leave them out of the table.

**Optima HIV (and Atomica).** Acts as contacts: the force of infection on population `g1` from `g2` through interaction type `i` (casual, regular, commercial, injecting) is built from the number of unprotected acts between the two populations per year, `m = n·(1 − e_κ·p_κ)`, the per-act probability weighted by prevalence and stage, and the effects of circumcision, STIs, PrEP and PEP, combined as `Λ = 1 − Π_g2 Π_i (1 − λ)`; "interaction type" names a partnership type, but no partner number or duration enters (Kerr CC, Stuart RM, Gray RT, et al. 2015, "Optima: a model for HIV epidemic analysis, program prioritization, and resource optimization", *JAIDS* 69(3):365–376, doi:10.1097/QAI.0000000000000605, supplement) [checked: supplement text]. Up to 14 populations by 21 health states. Accuracy rests on calibration. v4 runs this form today with `ss.contacts()` and beta per act.

**Goals (Avenir Health, in Spectrum/OneHealth).** The behavioural inputs are partners per year, sex acts per partner, condom use, and per-act probabilities by risk group (sex workers, clients, MSM, people who inject drugs, low-risk heterosexuals), with no partnership duration or concurrency parameter (Bhatnagar T, Dutta T, Stover J, et al. 2016, "Fitting HIV prevalence 1981 onwards for three Indian states using the Goals model and the Estimation and Projection Package", *PLoS One* 11(10):e0164001, PMC5053468) [checked]. That paper does not give the equation; I believe it is a Bernoulli form over partners and acts per partner, but I could not confirm it this session, so treat Goals' exact form as unverified.

**Thembisa (South Africa).** A deterministic cohort model stratified by risk group (high risk: propensity for concurrency or commercial sex; low risk: serially monogamous), sexual experience, marital status, and the risk group of the spouse. Short-term partnerships are the classical form: a probability per short-term partnership `1 − exp(−n·h·…)` from an average of 18 acts per partnership (3 a month for 6 months), times the rate of forming partnerships, times the average probability across partners' groups and ages. Marriages are a person state: transmission each month from the spouse's group at that group's prevalence, with monthly act rates, not tracking whether the spouse is infected. Sex work is a single act per contact (Johnson LF, Dorrington RE 2025, "Thembisa version 4.8: A model for evaluating the impact of HIV/AIDS in South Africa", technical report, University of Cape Town, sections 2 and 4) [checked: equations 4.13–4.20]. So the most detailed national HIV model in Africa uses Starsim's convention for short partnerships, and a person-level partnership state, not pairs, for long ones.

**EpiModel's `dcm`.** One- and two-group SI, SIR and SIS models parameterized by `inf.prob` (per act), `act.rate` (acts per person per unit time) and `balance` between the groups' acts; partnerships and their duration do not appear (EpiModel `param.dcm` documentation, CRAN) [checked]. EpiModel's authors put partnership structure in `netsim` (agents), not in `dcm`.

**gonovax (Whittles, Didelot, White; odin).** A deterministic compartmental gonorrhoea model with vaccination in odin, with low and high activity groups by partner change rate `p` and assortativity `epsilon`: `lambda[i] <- p[i] * beta * (epsilon * prop_C[i] + (1 - epsilon) * sum(foi_LH[]))`, with `foi_LH` weighting each group's prevalence by its partners (`mrc-ide/gonovax`, `inst/odin/model.R`) [checked: code]. So `beta` is per partnership and duration does not appear. It underlies Whittles LK, Didelot X, White PJ 2022, "Public health impact and cost-effectiveness of gonorrhoea vaccination: an integrated transmission-dynamic health-economic modelling analysis", *Lancet Infect Dis* 22(7):1030–1041, PMC9217755, described there as a deterministic compartmental model with low and high activity groups [checked: main text]. I found no general STI example in the odin or dust documentation beyond this.

**Other gonorrhoea, chlamydia, and syphilis compartmental models.** A systematic review of chlamydia models used for policy found 29 models in 47 publications, 20 deterministic compartmental and 9 individual-based (Rönn MM, Wolf EE, Chesson H, et al. 2017, "The use of mathematical models of chlamydia transmission to address public health policy questions: a systematic review", *Sex Transm Dis* 44(5):278–283) [checked: abstract]; the abstract does not say how many are pair models. Turner and colleagues' study of ethnic inequalities in gonorrhoea used a deterministic model stratified by sex, rate of partner change, and ethnic group [checked: DFID research-output summary, authors and venue not confirmed there; I believe it is Turner KME, Garnett GP, Ghani AC, Sterne JAC, Low N 2004, *Sex Transm Infect*, memory]. Grassly, Fraser and Garnett explain syphilis cycles with SIR models that deliberately leave out sexual behaviour (2005, "Host immunity and synchronized epidemics of syphilis across the United States", *Nature* 433:417–421) [checked: abstract and a secondary description]. White and colleagues' gonorrhoea models in Britain are, as I remember them, of the partner-change kind, but I did not check them this session.

**HPV.** Elbasha, Dasbach and Insinga's deterministic model (behind the Merck quadrivalent cost-effectiveness analyses) stratifies by sex, 17 age groups and three activity groups by partner change rate, and its force of infection depends on partnerships formed through a mixing matrix, the share of partners infected, and "the transmission probability per partnership" (2007, "Model for assessing human papillomavirus vaccination strategies", *Emerg Infect Dis* 13(1):28–41, technical appendix) [checked: appendix text]. Brisson's group's HPV-ADVISE is individual-based, with partnership formation and separation [checked: technical appendices exist; not read in detail], and their review of 16 HPV transmission models found predictions broadly concordant despite structural differences (Brisson M, et al. 2016, "Population-level impact, herd immunity, and elimination after human papillomavirus vaccination: a systematic review and meta-analysis of predictions from transmission-dynamic models", *Lancet Public Health* 1(1):e8–e17) [checked: abstract]. I could not confirm an earlier compartmental Brisson–Van de Velde model this session, so I leave it out.

**Kirby Institute.** Its HIV and STI models for Australia are a mix; the ones I found this session were individual-based (gonorrhoea in MSM) or a thesis-level deterministic compartmental model of heterosexual gonorrhoea, and Regan and Wilson have argued for simpler STI models for policy (Regan DG, Wilson DP 2008, "Modelling sexually transmitted infections: less is usually more for informing public health policy", *Trans R Soc Trop Med Hyg* 102(3):207–208) [checked: citation only]. I could not confirm how their compartmental models represent partnerships, so I make no claim.

**Skipped, as asked.** EMOD (individual relationships, no compartmental mode that I know of) and HIV Synthesis (individual-based).

## Uncertainty

The classification above rests on what the papers' abstracts, supplements, or code say. The weakest points are Goals' exact equation, Boily's Cotonou model, the Kirby models, and White's gonorrhoea models, which I could not check. None of them would change the conclusion: the partner-change model with a probability per partnership is the common convention, acts-as-contacts is the other common one, and pairs as compartments are the accurate but less used refinement for monogamy.
